Fossil Hunters

Dig site: Isle of Wight, England

The cliffs on the Isle of Wight are made of layers of rock. Each layer was laid down on top of the one before, so the layers near the top are younger and the ones near the bottom are older. The dinosaur bones here are all in one grey layer, the bone bed. It is about 125 million years old. Back then, floods on a wide river plain piled up plants and bones together.

Pick: tap to break up rock. Fast, but if you hit a bone you can see, it cracks, and a second hit breaks it. Brush: rub to sweep away loose bits. Very slow on solid rock, but safe near bones.

Dig in the bone bed. When the pick lands near a bone, little pale chips of bone appear and show you where to dig. When a bone sparkles, tap it to lift it out. A broken bone doesn't count!

The Isle of Wight dig site

The dinosaur cliffs are on the south-west coast of the Isle of Wight, between Compton Bay and Brighstone Bay. Their rocks are called the Wessex Formation. Rock layers build up one on top of another, so the youngest layers are at the top of the cliff and the oldest are at the bottom.

The rebuild table

Palaeontologists put bones back together to see the whole animal. The pale shadow on the table is the shape of the whole skeleton. Drag each bone from the tray onto the shadow, where its shape fits. You can also tap a bone, then tap where it goes. If a bone isn't in the right place, it slides back to the tray, so you can try again. The dotted shapes are bones still to place or still to find. Dig again to find more! The ruler underneath shows how long the animal was.
